To clarify under what conditions meanIGD may conceal
uneven compromises among DMs, this subsection examines
the search behaviors of representative MPMOP optimizers.
Asymmetric sacrifices are jointly determined by the geometry
of the party-wise Pareto sets and the selection mechanisms
used during optimization. We therefore categorize existing
MPMOP algorithms according to their principal search strate-
gies, including aggregate-objective optimization, multiparty
non-dominated sorting, party-by-party decomposition, and
archive- or clone-based search. For each category, we discuss
the circumstances under which its search process may lead
to unfair outcomes, and we use two representative cases to
illustrate the analysis.
For aggregate-objective methods such as OptAll, all party-
wise objectives are treated as a single objective vector. Con-
sequently, solutions favoring different DMs can remain non-
dominated in the concatenated objective space, even when
a solution is substantially inferior from the perspective of
one DM. This behavior is particularly likely when the party-
wise Pareto sets have limited overlap or are disjoint, since
improving the approximation quality for one DM may require
moving the population away from the preferred region of
another DM. Thus, a low meanIGD may be obtained by
combining a small loss for one DM with a much larger loss
for another.
Multiparty non-dominated sorting methods, including
OptMPNDS [1], OptMPNDS2 [2], and OptMPNDS3 [3],
reduce this risk by explicitly considering the Pareto status
of every DM. However, these methods mainly exploit ordinal
rank information. Hence, solutions with substantially different
sacrifice magnitudes may remain indistinguishable when their
party-wise rank vectors are mutually non-dominated. For ex-
ample, two solutions with rank vectors (1, 2) and (6, 1) can
receive comparable multiparty priority, although one solution
is much farther from the Pareto set of one DM. Therefore,
multiparty sorting improves party-wise Pareto consistency but
does not guarantee balanced approximation losses.
For party-by-party or decomposition-based search as
MOEA/D-MP [4], asymmetric sacrifices may arise when the
DMs have unequal approximation difficulty. When one DM
has a smooth and easily accessible Pareto structure, whereas
another has a disconnected, non-convex, or multimodal Pareto
structure, the population may converge more rapidly toward
the former. Although the objectives of all DMs are considered
sequentially, later updates may not fully compensate for the
slower progress on the more difficult party.
Archive- or clone-based methods like MPNNIA [5] may fur-
ther amplify an initial party-wise imbalance through unequal
survival or reproduction opportunities. In particular, if solu-
tions preferred by one DM consistently receive better ranks,
lower local density, or more cloning resources, that region can
progressively dominate the population. Such mechanisms may
preserve diversity within the favored region while reducing the
search effort devoted to the other DM.

S-V. VALIDITY OF NASH-PRODUCT EVALUATION OVER
NON-CONVEX UTILITY SETS
Classical Nash bargaining usually assumes convexity of the
feasible utility set when the Nash product is maximized to
obtain a bargaining solution with existence and uniqueness
guarantees. In the proposed framework, however, the Nash
product is used only to evaluate obtained solution sets rather
than to select a unique bargaining outcome. Therefore, the
attainable utility set is not required to be convex.

S-IX. MPMOP12-17 BENCHMARK PROBLEMS
This section presents detailed descriptions of the
MPMOP12–MPMOP17 benchmark problems, which are
specifically constructed to ensure that the PSs of different
DMs are mutually disjoint, i.e., no solution is Pareto-optimal
for all DMs simultaneously. This characteristic distinguishes
MPMOP12–MPMOP17 from MPMOP1–11, in which
common Pareto-optimal solutions across DMs may exist.
The detailed configurations of these benchmark problems are
summarized in Table S-VI.
To evaluate algorithmic behavior under varying levels of
compromise among DMs, multiple experimental scenarios are
further defined using different concession-rate thresholds. The
same set of concession-rate thresholds is applied consistently
to both categories of benchmark problems, enabling a unified
and fair comparison across problem types. The considered
threshold settings are reported in Table S-IV.
